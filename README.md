Hears Gray Birds’ Guodian Laozi: A Naïve Visual Translation Experiment, 1st Version
Introduction
The question behind this project is simple: what does the Guodian manuscript look like if we resist the temptation to reconstruct it into the received Laozi? I do not expect this experiment to produce a definitive translation. Rather, I wanted to document what happens when the manuscript's unusual graphs, variants, lexical possibilities, and ambiguities are allowed to remain unusual wherever I could justify doing so.
This project was mostly written after two passes of translation of the Guodian mostly “blind” to later received versions, so themes of the commentary are from the translation process itself. Readings emerged from the translation rather than my coming in with any assumptions or frameworks (at least not knowingly imposed). Chapter commentary was written directly after translating, with some notes taken directly after translating each chapter but the more complete version and synthesis crafted after translating all three bundles (A, B, and C). This commentary was without conscious influence from other specific reading or studying outside of background memory I double-checked, with cited exceptions. 
My commentary tends to be more thematic, and reference sections that may have become substantially before or after in the accepted sequence. The commentary is also fairly speculative and may reflect my own beliefs (and potentially opinions). As this project was not a rigorous academic project (or at least did not start with that intention), I did not rigorously “weed” such things out – nevertheless maybe they are helpful or at least interesting. I am presently working on commentary for the entirety of the received Laozi as well, but it is not included here yet. I hope that project will eventually have more thorough, subjective commentary for all chapters of the received (WangBi, potentially Mawangdui, potentially HeShangGong) and, treated separately, the Guodian.
Notes on received translation in the main text and the appendix: I did not consistently add Mawangdui variants in every chapter – I may go through at a later time to more comprehensively address the differences between Mawangdui and WangBi or HeShangGong, but the project is not currently at that stage.


First and foremost, I am not formally trained in Classical Chinese. I speak and write an intermediate level of Modern Chinese. I am not trained in paleography. I have read English translations of the Laozi/Dao De Jing, sure, but I don’t have them memorized, and most of what I had read was at least seven years before starting this project. I was taught some of the Old Chen Style Taijiquan sequence, and am trying to learn the rest on my own, but I am not trained in the “inner trainings” of Daoism to any deep degree. I am a hobbyist at best. I have a PhD, but it is in biology, earned by studying the behavior of birds. Perhaps one could say I am an “uncarved block”, ignorant. This work may reflect my ignorance, but it is sincere in its intention to understand the Guodian Laozi for what is written on it. In my ignorance, there may be things I misunderstand, or things I just don’t know about that would change the way I’ve written something here – please tell me. I may be particularly weak in my understanding of Classical Chinese Grammar, or established Chu script substitutions or conventions established in other documents, despite my best efforts. I am not pretending this is perfect; it is an attempt. That is all I have to say about my credibility, and, for the most part, myself. I invite you to view this as a “naïve visual readings experiment.” I wanted to see how this oldest known version of Laozi would read without trying to fit it with the later versions, to let it speak by itself.
I was inspired to do this project when I was reading Cook’s and Henricks’ work on the Guodian, and took a look at the differences they noted between the original characters and the ones in the received texts, most often Wang Bi. I was then inspired to resolve inconsistencies between the two by looking up photographs of the actual slips, from Chinese Text Project, DaoIsOpen.com, and “郭店楚墓竹簡 ( Chu Bamboo Slips from Guodian ) (荊門市博物館 , Jingmen City Museum)”. Upon seeing the photographs, I was inspired to try to transcribe and translate from scratch, using Wiktionary, Zi.Tools, the ShuoWen Jiezi, the ShuoWen Xinzheng by Ji Xuesheng, Paul Kroll’s “Student’s Dictionary of Classical and Medieval Chinese,” and finally Cook and Henricks for reference and guidance. This approach was to limit bias from later versions of the text from the Han and forward – I have some familiarity with the Dao De Jing from reading translations most of a decade ago, but I had not translated the Wang Bi before, nor do I have in-depth knowledge and memorization of these later texts. So, I dared myself to do this transcription and translation as “blind” to the WangBi and Mawangdui as possible, unless truly, truly stumped. I only translated the received (WangBi) Daodejing after completing an initial translation and at least one revision of the Guodian first. 
This is not an attempt to overturn the received tradition or to replace existing scholarly editions. Rather, it is an attempt to ask what the Guodian manuscript itself appears to say before later interpretive traditions are allowed to shape the translation.



My approach was as follows: 
•	Literal Reading: I assumed that the scribes meant every character they wrote in the most semantic sense, departing from this baseline only when a literal reading produced a grammatical impasse or broke structural coherence within the immediate syntax.. 
•	Dictionary Synthesis: If the literal sense did not work, then I would consult the ShuoWen Jiezi and ShuoWen Xinzheng (partially from zi.tools) to understand the connotations of the word, and known historical loans and substitutions. I would also consult Kroll’s dictionary for confirmation with translation. 
•	Component Deconstruction: If the character on the slips did not visually match (in my opinion) the characters from either Cook or Henricks, I tried to work out which radicals and components made up the character. I would then try to find a viable alternative supported by zi.tools or Wiktionary based on the character itself or how the components are expressed in characters that are found in those websites (if the one I figured was not already there). 
•	Phonetic Loans: I also investigated viable phonetic loans known from Warring States time period or since, though I never assume a phonetic loan right off the bat. I state that I have used a phonetic loan or component deconstruction in every case I have done it. I do not find rhyming schemes to be convincing evidence in general – I have trouble assuming everything is written to rhyme, and reconstructions, while rigorously researched, may still have error. 
•	Scholarly References: I also state when I cannot get what I see on the slip to agree well with any character I could find – in these cases, I will usually if not always default to what Cook or Henricks has listed. 
•	Damage and Loss: For characters that are faded, damaged, or missing due to broken or damaged slips (or simply unreadable due to the imperfect quality of the photographs and scans I have access to), I consult the same section in case there’s a pattern already strongly established. If this is not an obvious or convincing pattern, I will default again to the opinions of Henricks and Cook, but I will note it. I will footnote in every case a character is faded or damaged enough to make me doubt confident identification, and I will mark with a blank box where characters are altogether missing, and default again to Cook and Henricks to estimate how many are missing in each case. 
•	Variants: For any character that looks slightly weird, like an extra or missing stroke, I will note it, unless the variation in whether or not an “extra” mark exists is very well represented in zi.tools entries for the character (and even then, I may note it regardless).
I am also, for now, following the Jingmen City Museum 1998 arrangement and sequence of the bamboo slips in “Guodian Chumu Zhujian (郭店楚墓竹簡)”, although I recognize it is possible that these may not be the original order. Perhaps one day I will be brave and play with the order; not yet. 
A word of warning and a note on format: this translation leans heavily on its footnotes. Where a reading departs from convention, the footnote is where I show my work (e.g., the graph analysis, the sources I weighed, and where I'm genuinely unsure). Reading the body text alone will give my conclusions; reading the footnotes will show you why I got there, and where I could be wrong. Given how contested some of these graphs are, I'd consider the footnotes essential rather than optional. 
Also worth noting, footnotes recording my reasoning for translation decisions were written alongside the first-pass translation of each bundle, often moved to the meat of the commentary later. The bulk of the commentary itself was written afterward, once all three bundles (A, B, and C) had been translated at least once, which allowed me to write with the whole manuscript in view and to cross-reference related passages and graphs across bundles.
